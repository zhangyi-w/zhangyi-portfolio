Game project · Team build
Iterative game design under competition pressure
Built a game with friends inside a competition with hundreds of participants. Most teams went one of two ways: over-scoped and shipped something broken, or polished features no real player cared about. We took the third path — ran user interviews from the start of the build, every single week. Sat with real players, watched where they got stuck, where they got bored, where they lit up — and adjusted accordingly. Mechanics, difficulty curves, onboarding flow, all rebuilt against what the feedback actually said versus what we wished it said. The discipline of iterating on revealed-preference signal — not opinion — is what put us above the rest. Result: 2nd place. → Play it on itch.io
2nd place · Hundreds of participants · Iteration won the race
Family ed · Operator
Made them want to stay
My family founded and runs an education company that scaled to thousands of students. As it grew, a long-running safety problem emerged: kids wouldn't wait inside for parent pickup — they ran around outside, in the parking lot and the street. Real liability. The standard moves had all been tried — written rules, classroom reminders from teachers, stricter regulation, scolding when caught — and none of them stuck. Because none of them changed what kids actually wanted to do. Kids are kids; they'll do whatever's most interesting. I proposed the inverse: stop trying to force compliance, design the option they'd choose. We built a small play space at the school — toys, picture books, a TV running familiar shows. Within weeks, kids stayed inside on their own — not because they were told to, but because the option got better than running outside. Cheaper than hiring more security. A multi-year problem dissolved.
Pull, not push · 1000s of students · Multi-year issue solved
Family ed · Operator
Applied behavioral design (acquisition)
Same education company, different lever. Designed acquisition mechanics from real behavioral economics: gifting offers that triggered the endowment effect (once a parent saw the gift in their account, walking away felt like a loss), defaults set to lower friction at signup, pricing anchors that made discounts feel substantial. None of it was growth-hacking theatre — every choice was a deliberate call about what kind of decision the parent was actually making. Real customers, real revenue, real revealed-preference signal — not a dashboard.

Northeastern · RA (Qiang Wang)
Virtual interviewer that felt human
Built a high-fidelity virtual interviewer that interviewees couldn't easily distinguish from a real one. Pipeline: ChatTTS + CosyVoice voice cloning + Easy_Wav2Lip lip-sync, streamed over SSE off a SpringBoot backend. The product judgment: a virtual interviewer fails not on AI quality but on perceptual gaps — voice timing, lip-sync alignment, response latency. Bet the engineering effort on closing those gaps; the AI conversation itself took care of itself.

How I work a consumer bet

How I find a user pain point
I read for revealed preference, not stated preference — what people do, not what they say. The work is structured user research, not gut: I bring trained methodology to the problem, then frame what I find through motivation theory before writing a single line of product spec.
How AI integrates here I run AI-driven deep research at the front of every project — business reports, competitor scans, consumer-review synthesis at scale. AI compresses weeks of background reading into days and surfaces what to build, which problems to solve, and which mistakes to avoid.
Proves — taught + applied user research, not gut instinct dressed up as method
How I solve and refine it
Smallest shippable thing — architected before built, then refined relentlessly. I apply cognitive science, psychology, and UX research at every scale (data model to animation curve), mapping the user's mental model and attention flow, pruning the noises that pull focus.
How AI integrates here AI is part of the build itself, not adjacent to it. Daily Claude Code + MCP connectors.
Proves — architecture-first plus relentless refinement, at every scale
How I know it worked
Revealed-preference metrics — engagement, retention, referral — not vanity counts. If retention doesn't compound, scaling spend is the wrong move. I run the loop on evidence, not optimism.
How AI integrates here AI runs the measurement loop with me — gathering structured signal from qualitative feedback at scale, analyzing behavioral patterns across cohorts, drafting hypotheses for the next bet. The gap between "have data" and "know what it means" keeps shrinking.
Proves — retention & revealed-preference rigor, not dashboard theater
